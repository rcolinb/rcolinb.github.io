# N326 Exam 1: July 2026 comparison

## Bottom line

The July 2026 class scored **5.6 percentage points below the three previous classes pooled** (79.3% versus 84.9%). The size of that difference is educationally meaningful, and the lower performance was not caused only by one very low score. However, July included only nine students, so the exact size of the difference is uncertain.

The available evidence does **not** support a simple conclusion that the July exam form was defective or uniformly much harder:

- The July form's vendor/PDF difficulty was only 1–3 points lower than the earlier forms.
- Its point-biserial and discrimination summaries were as strong as the May form and slightly stronger than January and March.
- The broad content and response-format blueprints were similar.
- July students scored 7.7 points lower than earlier students on the 11 exact-common stems, which suggests that at least part of the difference traveled with the cohort, preparation, or instruction rather than only with new questions.

The best working interpretation is a **mixture of a lower-performing July cohort and some item/form-specific effects**, not a single-cause version problem.

## Class performance

| Session | Students | Mean | SD | Median | Range | At or above 75% |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Jan 2026 | 14 | 84.3% | 5.7% | 84.5% | 73.7–94.2% | 92.9% |
| Mar 2026 | 19 | 84.8% | 4.5% | 86.0% | 73.9–91.8% | 94.7% |
| May 2026 | 11 | 85.8% | 6.7% | 86.2% | 76.5–97.9% | 100.0% |
| **Jul 2026** | **9** | **79.3%** | **6.9%** | **79.9%** | **64.7–86.7%** | **88.9%** |

July's nine scores ranged from 64.7% to 86.7%. Excluding the lowest score raises the July mean only to 81.1%, still below every previous class mean. This looks more like a generally lower distribution than a single-outlier artifact.

### Statistical comparisons

| Comparison | Mean difference, July minus comparison | Welch 95% CI | Welch p | Hedges g |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Prior sessions pooled | −5.6 points | −11.0 to −0.2 | .045 | −0.97 |
| March | −5.5 points | −11.0 to 0.0 | .051 | −0.99 |
| May | −6.5 points | −12.9 to 0.0 | .049 | −0.92 |
| January | −5.0 points | −10.9 to 0.9 | .090 | −0.78 |

The effect estimates are consistently large and negative, but the intervals are wide. When the four Welch tests are treated as one family, the Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-values are .068–.090. Therefore, the responsible conclusion is **a strong descriptive signal worth acting on and monitoring, not definitive proof of a persistent class or form effect**.

## Were the exam versions materially different?

### Form-level characteristics

| Session | Questions | Total points | Vendor/PDF difficulty | Avg. point-biserial | Discrimination index |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| January | 58 | 140 | .78 | .20 | .20 |
| March | 58 | 140 | .76 | .21 | .22 |
| May | 56 | 140 | .77 | .23 | .25 |
| **July** | **56** | **140** | **.75** | **.23** | **.25** |

The July form was slightly harder by the vendor/PDF metric, but a 1–3 point difference is much smaller than the observed 5–6.5 point class-score difference. Its discrimination indicators were not worse.

The format mix also remained close:

- January: 46 multiple choice, 6 fill-in-the-blank, 1 matching, 4 matrix, and 1 bow-tie.
- March: 45 multiple choice, 5 fill-in-the-blank, 1 ordering, 6 matrix, and 1 bow-tie.
- May: 43 multiple choice, 6 fill-in-the-blank, 1 ordering, 5 matrix, and 1 bow-tie.
- July: 45 multiple choice, 4 fill-in-the-blank, 6 matrix, and 1 bow-tie.

At the broad topic level, July was also close to the earlier forms. The clearest count difference was one psychosis item and 11 psychopharmacology items in July, versus 12 psychopharmacology items and no psychosis item on the recent comparison form. This is too small to explain the overall score gap by itself.

### Common-item evidence

Only **11 of the 56 July items** had an exact normalized-stem match on an earlier Exam 1. Those common items accounted for 27.5 of 140 points; the remaining 45 items accounted for 112.5 points. All 11 retained the same normalized key, and 10 retained the same normalized response set across every matched prior administration; Q40 had a response-set variation. Thus, the forms were similar in coarse blueprint but not highly parallel at the individual-item level.

| Item set | July weighted facility | Earlier weighted facility on same stems | Difference |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 11 exact-common stems | 70.7% | 78.4% | −7.7 points |
| 45 July-unique stems | 81.4% | — | — |

The common-item result matters: July students performed worse even when the stem—and usually the response set—was held constant. This weakens the claim that new July items alone caused the lower class average. It does not fully eliminate a form effect because item ordering, surrounding context, administration conditions, or instruction could still differ.

The largest common-stem declines were:

| July item | Focus | July facility | Earlier facility | Difference |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Q49 | Alcohol withdrawal: anxiety/tremor action | 55.6% | 81.8% | −26.3 points |
| Q22 | Lithium liquid-dose calculation | 66.7% | 90.9% | −24.2 points |
| Q42 | Priority during a therapeutic-milieu interaction | 44.4% | 63.6% | −19.2 points |
| Q38 | Working phase of the nurse–client relationship | 77.8% | 90.9% | −13.1 points |
| Q40 | Nontherapeutic communication technique | 55.6% | 66.7% | −11.1 points |

Before revising these items, review the full options and keyed answers. A decline across cohorts can reflect instruction/readiness, but it can also reveal an option that depends on a subtle wording distinction.

Two useful cautions:

1. The largest deficits—dissociative disorders and medication calculation—each rest on only two July items. They are good targets for a quick diagnostic check, not proof of a broad curricular failure.
2. July was actually stronger in psychopharmacology overall. The lithium-calculation decline is therefore more consistent with a calculation/application gap than with a general medication-knowledge gap.

## Recommended response

1. **Do a short, low-stakes check before changing the course or curving the exam.** Include a novel medication calculation, one dissociation discrimination item, and two crisis/legal priority items. This will show whether the gaps generalize beyond the original questions.
2. **Review the three common items that declined by at least 15 points.** Check the key, option parallelism, whether more than one answer can be defended, and whether the item tests a decision rule students were explicitly taught.
3. **Use Quiz 3 to strengthen transfer to Exam 2.** Keep direct suicide assessment, plan/means/immediacy, safety planning, bipolar versus unipolar treatment implications, and medication adverse-effect escalation. These require priority decisions rather than recall alone.
4. **Add one fresh lithium calculation if medication math is within the Quiz 3 blueprint.** Do not reuse the 600 mg/300 mg per 5 mL item verbatim; use a new dose and concentration so it measures transfer.
5. **Preserve stable item IDs and version links.** Record whether a change affects only punctuation, the options/key, or the construct. This will make future common-item equating substantially stronger than normalized-stem matching.
6. **Wait for another outcome before diagnosing a cohort problem.** Quiz 3 and Exam 2 can show whether the July shift persists, narrows after targeted instruction, or was specific to Exam 1 content.

## Limitations

- July has only nine students, and all cohorts are small.
- Students were not randomly assigned to sessions or exam forms.
- The common-item method matches normalized stems; it cannot guarantee identical options, keys, sequencing, or administration conditions.
- Topic results mix items of different point values and formats and are descriptive rather than causal.
- Multiple statistical comparisons increase false-positive risk; adjusted results are reported for that reason.
- Results describe these classes and should not be treated as universal item parameters without more administrations.
